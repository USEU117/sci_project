Automated visual inspection is essential to modern manufacturing, where defects may appear as small texture disruptions, scratches, deformations, missing components, or other category-specific deviations. Building supervised detectors for this setting is difficult because defective products are rare, defect taxonomies evolve, and pixel-level annotation is costly. Industrial anomaly detection therefore commonly learns normal appearance and identifies deviations at test time, as exemplified by benchmarks such as MVTec AD, VisA, MPDD, and BTAD \cite{bergmann2019mvtec,zou2022spot,jezek2021mpdd,mishra2021vtadl}. In practice, however, even defect-free reference images may be limited when a new product variant or production line is introduced. This motivates normal-only few-shot anomaly detection, in which a model must detect and localize previously unseen defects from only one, two, or four normal reference images.

Patch-based methods offer a practical solution to this problem. Rather than fitting a category-specific classifier, they represent normal reference images as local features and score a query patch by its discrepancy from a normal memory. PatchCore established the effectiveness of nearest-neighbor patch memories for industrial anomaly localization \cite{roth2022patchcore}. More recently, strong frozen visual foundation models have improved the quality of such local representations. DINOv2 provides robust self-supervised visual features \cite{oquab2024dinov2}, and AnomalyDINO showed that these features can support competitive patch-based few-shot anomaly detection without category-specific parameter training \cite{damm2025anomalydino}. Training-free alternatives such as SubspaceAD further demonstrate that simple statistical modeling over frozen DINOv2 patches can be highly competitive \cite{lendering2026subspacead}. These results make a compelling case for strong visual representations, but they do not imply that one representation is uniformly optimal for every industrial texture, object geometry, and defect morphology.

In parallel, vision-language pretraining has introduced representations with different invariances and semantic biases. CLIP learns transferable image and text embeddings from large-scale image-text supervision \cite{radford2021clip}. Its use in industrial anomaly detection has led to zero- and few-shot methods including WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, and PromptAD \cite{jeong2023winclip,zhou2024anomalyclip,li2024promptad}. Although these methods are often discussed through their language or prompt components, their image encoders also provide visual patch features shaped by a training objective that differs substantially from self-supervised DINOv2. This raises a narrower question that does not require text at inference: can two frozen visual patch spaces provide complementary evidence when they are evaluated through the same normal-reference anomaly detector?

Recent work has already combined CLIP and DINO-family features, so encoder pairing alone is not a sufficient novelty claim. Sea-CLIP uses CLIP and DINOv2 features together with trainable prompts, synthetic anomalies, and a learnable anomaly matching decoder \cite{guo2026seaclip}. PAPL combines CLIP and DINO features within a zero-shot prompt-learning and hierarchical multi-scale framework \cite{ma2026papl}, while another zero-shot framework employs trained dual-modality attention and stabilized pooling \cite{jiang2025clipdino}. ReMP-AD also integrates normal-reference retrieval with vision-language prior fusion for few-shot inspection \cite{ma2025rempad}. These methods pursue high-capacity learned fusion or prompting. Consequently, their final performance does not isolate whether a second frozen image representation, without a learned fusion module, yields stable improvements over a matched single-encoder detector.

This distinction matters for both scientific interpretation and deployment. A learned fusion network can exploit auxiliary data, synthetic anomalies, prompts, or query-dependent adaptation, making it difficult to attribute gains to intrinsic representation complementarity. It can also introduce protocol questions when validation anomalies or test-derived statistics influence model selection. Conversely, a fixed fusion rule is transparent and easy to reproduce, but its average gains may hide category-specific negative transfer and substantial computational overhead. Existing comparisons rarely treat the following elements as a single controlled question: an otherwise identical normal-memory pipeline, multiple reference-image seeds and shot counts, frozen cross-dataset evaluation, complete image- and pixel-level metrics, explicit failure boundaries, and a replayable evidence package.

We therefore study a deliberately minimal dual-encoder visual baseline. Patch features are extracted from a frozen DINOv2 ViT-B/14 encoder and the frozen image tower of AnomalyCLIP ViT-L/14@336. The AnomalyCLIP branch is used only as an image encoder; no text embedding or language score is computed during inference. Its patch grid is bilinearly aligned to the DINOv2 grid, the two branches are independently L2-normalized, and their equally weighted features are concatenated and normalized again. A category-specific memory bank is built exclusively from a few normal reference images, and each query patch is scored by its nearest-neighbor distance. To isolate the effect of the additional representation, we compare this model with a matched DINO-only control that uses the same references, nearest-neighbor scoring, post-processing, and evaluation protocol.

We evaluate the frozen method on MPDD, BTAD, VisA, and MVTec AD using three reference-image seeds and 1-, 2-, and 4-shot settings, for a total of 36 dataset-level configurations. Relative to the matched DINO-only control, the dual-encoder representation improves mean Pixel-AP by 0.0258 on MPDD, 0.0249 on BTAD, 0.0524 on VisA, and 0.0320 on MVTec AD. The gain is positive in all nine seed/shot configurations of every dataset. Nevertheless, the effect is not universal: BTAD exhibits lower image-level AP and F1-max, and persistent category-level losses occur for examples such as MVTec leather and VisA chewinggum. The second encoder also increases inference cost, with the current implementation reaching approximately 2.41 images per second in the measured steady-state setting. We report these limitations as part of the main result rather than treating them as incidental exceptions.

The contributions of this work are threefold. First, we provide a matched, training-free study that isolates the contribution of adding a heterogeneous frozen visual patch representation to a DINOv2 normal-memory detector. Second, we evaluate complementarity across four industrial datasets and 36 seed/shot configurations, reporting complete metrics, bootstrap intervals, and category-level negative transfer instead of only aggregate improvements. Third, we release a reproducibility-oriented evidence package containing an exact method specification, split and checkpoint hashes, compact prediction maps, table-reconstruction scripts, leakage checks, efficiency measurements, and failure-sample identifiers. Taken together, the study establishes a transparent reference point for when simple frozen feature fusion helps industrial anomaly localization, when it does not, and what it costs.
